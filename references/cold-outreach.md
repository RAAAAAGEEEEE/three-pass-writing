# Cold outreach

First contact with someone who did not ask to hear from you. The deepest profile in this skill, because it is the format where an invented claim costs the most and where a generic message is invisible.

Applies to cold email, LinkedIn DM, X DM, and the first message in any channel.

## Before writing anything

Three questions. If the user cannot answer them, the message is not the problem.

1. **Who is the reader, precisely?** Role, company size, what they are responsible for this quarter. "B2B agencies" is a market, not a reader. The reader is "the person who runs delivery at a 5-person web agency and just took on more clients than their process handles".
2. **What signal made them a target?** Something specific and verifiable: they posted about the problem, they run a stack the product plugs into, they published a job ad for the work the product removes. A message with no signal is a broadcast, and reads like one.
3. **What can be proved?** The numbers, demo, or artefact that survives "prove it". No proof means a softer promise, not a louder one.

## Structure

The default, which works and is worth beating rather than replacing:

```
Subject     3–5 words, lowercase, concrete, no promise
Line 1      the signal: why them, specifically, verifiably
Line 2–3    the pain, in their words, not the product's
Line 4–5    what the product does about it: one sentence, one proof
Last line   one ask, small, with zero setup cost
```

**Target: 80–120 words.** Every word past 120 lowers the reply rate for the same content. Under 50 usually means the signal or the proof was dropped.

Variants worth testing in pass 1's alternative openings:
- **Signal-first**: quote the trigger, then the offer. Highest reply rate when the signal is real.
- **Result-first**: the outcome for a comparable company, then the ask. Requires a quotable result.
- **Question-first**: one specific question about their process. Lowest commitment, best for starting a conversation rather than booking a call.

## The ask

One ask. Rank by cost to the reader:

1. A yes/no question they can answer in four words. Cheapest, highest reply rate.
2. Permission to send something ("want the teardown?"). Earns a second message.
3. A link to a 60-second demo. Fine when the demo is genuinely short.
4. A meeting. Most expensive; keep it for warm or high-intent readers.

Never stack two. "Reply yes, or book a call here, or check out our site" is three asks and reads as desperation.

## Should you give something away?

Yes, when it is specific to *that* reader and costs them nothing to consume: a teardown of their page, a checklist for their exact stack, a two-minute recording of the problem happening on their site. It changes the message from "buy this" to "here, this is yours".

No, when it is a generic PDF. A generic lead magnet in a cold email is another ask disguised as a gift, and readers know the difference.

Best structure when giving something: describe it in one line, ask permission to send it, deliver it in the reply. The permission step is what makes the second message welcome instead of intrusive.

## Personalisation that is not creepy

Use: what they published (posts, docs, job ads, pricing page, changelog), what their product does, publicly visible tech choices.

Do not use: personal details found by aggregating sources, family, location beyond a business address, anything from a data broker, anything that makes the reader wonder how you know it.

The test: if the reader asks "how do you know that?", the answer must be a public link they would expect you to have seen.

## Compliance: write it in, do not work around it

Depending on the reader's jurisdiction, cold B2B email is regulated (GDPR/ePrivacy in the EU, CAN-SPAM in the US, PECR in the UK, CASL in Canada, and others). The rules differ, they change, and this file is not legal advice, but the following belong in the message and cost nothing:

- Real sender identity: name, company, and a valid postal or business address where required.
- A working, one-click way to opt out, honoured immediately.
- A truthful subject line that matches the body.
- A defensible reason this specific person was contacted, in the message itself.
- Nothing scraped from a source whose terms forbid it, and no purchased lists.

This skill will not write anything designed to evade spam filters, disguise the sender, or fake a prior relationship. Those tactics are what get a sending domain burned, and they are the opposite of what makes cold outreach work.

## Deliverability, honestly

Copy is not the lever people think it is. Reply rate collapses from bad targeting and bad list hygiene long before wording matters. What the copy controls:

- One link at most in a first message; zero is often better.
- No attachments.
- No tracking pixel in a first message: it earns nothing and costs trust.
- Plain text, no image-only body, no heavy formatting.
- The same message readable on a phone in five seconds.

## Follow-ups

- Two follow-ups at most, four to seven days apart, then stop.
- Each one adds something new: a different angle, a new proof, a smaller ask. Never "just bumping this".
- Shorter each time. The third message should be one or two lines.
- After the last one, say it is the last one and mean it.

## What pass 2 checks hardest here

- The signal in line 1 is verifiable and correctly attributed. Getting the reader's own facts wrong is the most expensive error in the format: it is a P0.
- No claimed result, customer, or number that the writing context cannot back.
- One ask.
- Under 120 words.
- Nothing that presumes a relationship that does not exist ("following up on our conversation", "as discussed").
- The reader could plausibly answer without a call.
